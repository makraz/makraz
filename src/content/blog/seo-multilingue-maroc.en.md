A Moroccan site that wants to be found has to deal with something few SEO tools anticipate: the same customer may search in French in the morning and in Arabic in the evening, using technical vocabulary borrowed from English. Translating the same page three times does not solve this — and can make it worse.

## Pick the languages that matter, not every language you could have

The first decision is not a technical one. A Marrakech clinic, a Casablanca law firm and an agri-food exporter do not share an audience: the first needs Arabic and French, the second mostly French, the third English above all.

Adding a language costs more than its initial translation. Every extra page has to be maintained, updated and corrected each time the offer changes. An English version last touched two years ago tells a visitor something about you — and tells Google the same thing. Two languages kept current beat three where one is rotting.

## One URL per language, never a JavaScript toggle

This is the most common and most expensive mistake. A language switcher that swaps the text without changing the address leaves a single document to index: search engines see one version, ignore the others, and all that translation work stays invisible.

Each language needs its own URL — `/fr/`, `/ar/`, `/en/` — and every page must declare its counterparts with `hreflang` tags, including an `x-default` for visitors whose language you have not planned for. The same goes for the sitemap. It is the only way to tell a search engine that these pages are not competing with each other but corresponding to each other.

## Write for how people actually search

The vocabulary of Moroccan queries is not the vocabulary of brochures. People search for "psychomotricienne Marrakech" rather than "psychomotor care", "prix site web Maroc" rather than "our pricing". Many searches mix languages, and Arabic is frequently typed in Latin characters.

That does not mean writing badly. It means naming things the way a customer names them at least once per page — in the title, in a subheading, in an FAQ answer — then developing the point in the careful language that earns trust. A page that speaks Arabic to an Arabic-speaking audience, rather than being a word-for-word rendering of the French, ranks better because it genuinely is better.
